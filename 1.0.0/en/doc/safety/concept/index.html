    0. How to read this document, and what it deliberately does not do¶

    The standard and this document. ISO 26262:2018 is licensed to the user since 2026-09-22; it is not part of the repository. The comparison of neXtBSW with it is the gap analysis iso26262-gap.md (not published, available on request) (SYS-58). This document is written in the vocabulary the field has made common (SEooC, ASIL, assumption of use, safe state, fault tolerant time interval, fault handling time interval, diagnostic coverage, freedom from interference, tool confidence level) and explains each term in its own words where it is first used. Where it refers to the standard it cites part and clause only (for example "6-7.4.9" = ISO 26262-6:2018, clause 7.4.9) and never reproduces its text, tables or figures; the requirements behind such a reference are in the gap analysis in its own words. The questions issues 1 and 2 had to leave open for lack of the standard are answered there (section 9.4); the gap analysis's proposals for this document and their disposition are listed in section 9.6. Issue 3 (C1, decision ZH of CM-07): until issue 2 this paragraph said that the standard was not available and that the document contains no clause references.

    No invented numbers. This concept states no failure rates, no FIT values and no diagnostic coverage percentages. Every time given here is a constant of the code (and the file it comes from is named), a configured value of the project, a value derived from those (the derivation is named), or a measurement with its date and record. Where a number would be needed but does not exist, the entry says so. The diagnostic coverage of each stage-1 mechanism is rated as an ordinal class in the failure analysis fmea.md (SYS-20); for the device drivers it is the later stage SYS-28, which additionally needs manufacturer documents that we do not have (section 13).

    What this document is. The concept level: what the element is, what it assumes, which mechanisms exist, what they react to and how fast, and where the boundary to non-qualified third-party software runs. It is the input for the failure analysis (SYS-20), the freedom-from-interference work (SYS-21), the requirements with traceability (SYS-22) and the safety manual for integrators (DOC-19).

    What this document is not. It is not a safety case, not a proof of compliance, and not a promise that an ECU built on neXtBSW is ASIL B capable. neXtBSW is a hobby project; nothing here has been assessed by anyone outside it.

    1. The element¶

    1.1 What "out of context" means here¶

    neXtBSW is developed without knowing the vehicle, the ECU or the hazard it will be part of. An element built that way makes its assumptions explicit instead: it states which safety goals it assumes the item has, which ASIL it aims at, and which duties it hands to the integrator. The integrator then checks the assumptions against his own item and either accepts them or replaces them — and where he replaces one, the mechanisms in section 6 have to be re-evaluated for his case.

    This is exactly the shape of the rest of the project: the architecture already refuses to let the eval board decide anything (CLAUDE.md, guiding principle), configuration is separated from code in three levels, and every module declares what it needs and what it provides. The safety concept is the same idea applied to the hazard side: the element declares what it needs from its context.

    1.2 Scope: what is part of the element¶

    The user decided on 2026-09-21 (decision board safety-umfang): everything, including the device drivers — not only the core. The element is therefore the whole of neXtBSW as it is built into an ECU image, plus the tools that produce parts of that image.

    With the acceptance of issue 1 (abn-sys19, same day) the user staged that scope: the safety argument for ASIL B is built from mechanisms of the base software alone (stage 1, now); the device drivers' own safety mechanisms follow later and encapsulated (stage 2, SYS-28). The device drivers stay in the table below, because their code runs in the image and the rules of stage 1 apply to it (static analysis, freedom from interference, the safe_off declaration of their outputs), but their diagnostics are not credited in stage 1. Section 1.4 draws the line.

    1.3 Platform status¶

    The concept is written for the s32k1xx family, because that is the only family with a safety port today. The mechanisms of section 6 are split deliberately into a family-neutral part (the supervisor, its events and reactions, the safe outputs table semantics, the RTE deadlines, E2E, ecuState) and a family part (MPU, ERM/ECC, CRC, LPIT deadman, SIM supply monitor, watchdog registers). For stm32, s32k3xx and rm4x the family part does not exist yet; an integrator on those families gets the neutral part and nothing else. That is a gap, not a silence — see section 9.2.

    1.4 Two stages: base-software mechanisms now, device-driver mechanisms later¶

    The line between the two stages follows who detects and who executes, not which file a line of code lives in:

    Stage 1 — base software (credited now) Stage 2 — device drivers (later, encapsulated, not credited now)
    mechanisms whose detection runs on the MCU and uses only the MCU, its peripherals and the configuration: supervisor, watchdog, MPU, ECC, ROM check, supply and reference supervision, register lock/check, halt deadman, fault handlers, RTE deadlines, E2E, signal qualifier, CAN gate, ecuState, bootloader image check diagnostics that a part outside the MCU performs or reports over a bus, and the driver rules that react to them: SM-40 (TMC6200), SM-41 (MC33771C), SM-42 (UJA113x), SM-43 (BTS5030), and the device-dependent parts of SM-33 (transceiver status through the SBC) and SM-52 (SBC reset counter)
    the safe outputs table and its walk (SM-23 … SM-25) — including the entries a device driver declares with safe_off, because the table walk writes MCU port registers and needs no driver code anything that needs the driver to run, a bus to work or the part to answer in order to take effect

    The consequence is deliberate and stated per goal in section 2.2: a safety goal whose detection lives in a part outside the MCU (cell voltages, balancing) has no stage-1 coverage, and the gap is named (section 9.5), not filled with driver diagnostics. The interface through which stage 2 will report into the base software — a fault or qualifier word to the safe supervisor — is itself a base-software mechanism and is proposed for stage 1 (gap G5, section 10.9).

    2. Assumed safety goals and target ASIL¶

    2.1 Target ASIL¶

    ASIL B (user, decision board safety-asil, 2026-09-21), confirmed for the first implementation with the acceptance of issue 1 (abn-sys19). First intended applications named by the user: motor control and battery management.

    Consequences taken in this concept:

    • Freedom from interference between safety-related and QM software is treated as required — but without the independence and decomposition arguments that a higher ASIL would need. The work is SYS-21; today all tasks share one address space and the MPU protects only the safety manager's data (section 6.3).
    • Structural coverage at unit level (proposal C5): at ASIL B the standard expects statement and branch coverage strongly and recommends modified condition/decision coverage (MC/DC), which it expects strongly only at ASIL D; it sets no number, but a missing or unjustified target counts as insufficient (6-9.4.4, in the reading of iso26262-gap.md (not published, available on request) section 4 point 7). The measurement infrastructure exists (TOOL-40) and reports statement and branch coverage against thresholds per module (tools/test/coverage.yaml); the path to MC/DC is named there for the case of a higher ASIL. Enforcing the thresholds with a rationale per shortfall is proposal C17 (task DOC-52, after release R1). Issue 3: issue 2 called MC/DC "desirable but not required" and left out a clause reference "because the standard was not available".
    • A single ECU without a redundant channel is assumed. No lockstep core, no second MCU voting — the S32K1 has neither, and the multi-MCU work (ARCH-47) is about flashing, not about redundancy.

    ASIL C and D are to be considered later (user, abn-sys19). The element makes no claim for them today, but the user's decision asks the base software to implement its mechanisms "um ASIL B (oder höher) erreichen zu können" — so no mechanism should be built in a way that closes the path upwards. What a later stage for C/D would have to add is kept visible rather than struck out:

    • independence of the supervising channel from the supervised one — today supervision runs in the same core and the same clock domain (AoU-13), which is the main single-channel limit;
    • decomposition arguments between partitions, on top of the partitioning of SYS-21;
    • the MC/DC path of TOOL-40, named there and not built;
    • the replacement of the non-qualified RTOS, compiler and libraries (section 8) rather than their argument from use.

    Which mechanisms would have to be replaced rather than extended for C/D is a question for the later stage; the single-channel supervision inside one core and the non-qualified RTOS and compiler are the known candidates. AoU-03 carries the integrator's side.

    2.2 Assumed safety goals¶

    These are assumptions about the item, not statements about a real vehicle. Each is written so that it can be checked off or replaced by the integrator. The element does not fulfil any of them by itself; it contributes the mechanisms listed in the column "stage 1".

    Strict separation (user, abn-sys19). Only base-software mechanisms (section 1.4) are credited. The column "later device contribution" names what the device drivers will add in stage 2 (section 13); it is not part of the argument today and must not be counted by SYS-20, SYS-22 or DOC-19. Where the base software alone does not cover a goal, the column "coverage at stage 1" says so and points to a gap in section 9.5.

    The goals (ASG-01, ASG-02, ASG-03, ASG-04, ASG-05, ASG-06 and ASG-07, all ASIL B assumed) are the nodes of goals.sdoc (moved from this table by DOC-21). Each node holds the goal, the ASIL assumed, the base-software mechanisms credited at stage 1 (field STAGE1_CONTRIBUTION), the coverage at stage 1 (COVERAGE with the lead word covered / limited / partial / none and COVERAGE_NOTE with the full statement and the gaps of section 9.5) and the later device contribution, which is not credited (LATER_CONTRIBUTION).

    What changed against issue 1: ASG-01, ASG-03 and ASG-04 were argued with SM-40 and SM-41 there; under the strict separation they lose that support, and the honest result is one partial and two uncovered goals. ASG-07 was argued with a run-time ROM check that — as SYS-23 found — never runs. The mechanism numbers in this table were also corrected: issue 1 cited SM-20/21/22 for the safe outputs, the fault handlers and the halt deadman (catalogue: SM-23, SM-17, SM-06), SM-13 for the register lock and check (catalogue: SM-21/SM-22) and SM-11 for the ROM check (catalogue: SM-14).

    If an integrator's safety goal requires the function to keep working (a fail-operational goal) rather than to stop, neXtBSW supports it only within the limits of AoU-04 and AoU-32 (SYS-117): for the events the supervisor declares degradable — faults of one attached device and, since SYS-135, the trip of a hardware shut-off path (SM-58, report HW_SHUTOFF), never a fault of the MCU itself and never a finding of the shut-off path — a project may switch only the affected safe-output groups and keep the rest running (SD, section 3). A function that must continue after a fault of the MCU is not supported; every other reaction stays fail-safe — de-energise, or reset, or both. Issue 3 (CM-07 decision ZA, F-01): the text released in review SYS-117 (R8) named the device fault only; the trip of a hardware shut-off path is the second case, decided by the user in the safety review of SYS-128/SYS-130 (C1).

    3. Safe states¶

    neXtBSW knows two reactions of its own, plus "report and continue" — and, since SYS-23, a held variant of the first (S1 held, below), decided by the user on 2026-09-21 (sys23-halt, option a; corrected by DOC-48, issue 2 called the decision still open). Since SYS-117 a project may choose a fifth, SD — degraded, for the few events the supervisor declares degradable (below).

    S1 — de-energised outputs ("safe state"). The generated safe outputs table of the project is walked: every PWM group of the project is forced to its inactive level through the FTM software output control, and every output that a module manifest declares connect.<name>.safe_off (for example the enable of a gate driver) is driven inactive. The walk needs no stack, no RAM and no driver object, it skips a peripheral whose clock is gated, and it ends with a data synchronisation barrier — so it is callable from a task, from the supervisor and from an interrupt (platforms/s32k1xx/hardFaultHandler/include/hardFaultHandler/safeOutputs.h, SYS-06/IO-32).

    S1 is a one-way street upwards. leaveSafeState() releases only the indicator output (an LED on the eval board). No power output is switched back on there, because the safe-state code does not know the state a driver would have to return to. A power stage comes back only through an explicit enable() of its own driver. The indicator comes back with the level the application last wrote to it, which is why it must be no power output and should not be written by the application at all (AoU-30, SYS-87).

    On a board without power outputs the table is empty and entering S1 writes no peripheral register at all. That is the board-neutral case and it is covered by the second build profile (buildtest_s32k148_min, NX_SAFE_OUTPUT_COUNT 0).

    Selected safe state of a PWM group (SYS-116, decisions C1 … C6 of 2026-09-29). "Inactive level" above is freewheel, and it stays the only safe state of every group whose project lists no active short. A project may allow per group active_short_low and/or active_short_high (pwm.<group>.safe_states, freewheel always listed); the application selects the state in force at run time through a service that accepts only an allowed, not withdrawn state. Every walk of the table - S1, S1 held, SD, the fault handlers, the halt deadman - then writes, per entry of the group, the value of the selected state: for the timer the short pattern (all channels forced, the low-side resp. high-side ones to their active level, before dead-time insertion), for the gate-driver enable bound to the group a store that leaves it as it is. The selection word is read once per entry from ordinary RAM and must hold exactly the pattern of an allowed short; any other value (0 after a reset, a corrupted word, the pattern of a short the group does not allow) and a gated clock of the group's timer give freewheel. A bridge fault of a device bound to the group withdraws the short for the power cycle before any walk of the reaction (decision C4, in the safe supervisor since SYS-140, NX_SAFE_BRIDGE_FAULT_CLASSES, SSR-091), the family port keeps the short in S1, in a degraded group and after a debug halt (C2, SYS-139, NX_SAFE_STATE_SHORT_HOLD, SSR-092), and the hardware fault input of the timer forces freewheel by itself (SYS-85, SM-58). Every reaction that resets ends the short: during reset and restart the bridge is in the board's reset state (C3, AoU-33 (c)). With both code facts in the tree nxconfig accepts an active short (since SYS-139/SYS-140, 7f82689e, b9eac59c, 2026-09-30; before it refused every short). The application requests the state through the RTE port safety.safe_state.<GROUP>, a refused request leaves the selection as it is and is reported (C6, SYS-141). The integrator's side is AoU-33 (section 4.3).

    Start-up test of the active short (SM-27, SYS-141/SYS-143). RteSystem::init() builds every short whose project enables the test once, before the init runnables, only after a power-on: the pattern is forced and read back from the timer, the state found before is restored and read back, and the gate-driver enable bound to the group must read off before and after (read through the driver). After any other reset the outcome of the power-on test is taken from a record in no-init RAM; without an intact record of a passed test the group has freewheel only until the next power-on. The hold is a busy wait whose sum over all groups tools/rtegen bounds to a third of the MCU watchdog time-out. A failed test removes the shorts of the group until the next power-on; the phase voltages (IO-64) are not measured. The integrator's side is AoU-38.

    Not credited for release R1. SM-26 and SM-27 are described and host verified (safe-state-select, short-hold, safe-supervisor-bridge-fault, nxconfig-safestate, the start-test tests of tools/rtegen); their board proof is open (fmea.md SM-26, SM-27, B12 … B14). Decision ZD of CM-07: for R1 they are not credited (CREDITED: no in mechanisms.sdoc, as the safety manual already reads them); the requirements SSR-089 … SSR-092 stay and specify SM-23 or SM-34 (section 6.5). Issue 3 (F-02, F-04): issue 2 called AoU-33 "announced" and the RTE port a follow-up here, and credited SM-26/SM-27 in the catalogue.

    S2 — software system reset. softwareSystemReset() after enterLimpHome() and after S1. A reset is a reaction, not a repair: what happens afterwards depends on the board. With a system basis chip in the loop, the SBC counts resets (RCC) and goes to forced sleep on the fourth, which removes the MCU supply until a wake-up; on the eval board that path is armed only with JP1 closed. The ECU state management reads the start reason from two sources since SYS-18 and can be configured to go to sleep after a watchdog reset (modules.ecuState.after_watchdog_reset: sleep).

    S1 held — safe state held without reset (SYS-23). For a fault that a reset cannot remove, S1 + S2 would only restart into the same fault, in a loop. SafeSupervisor::holdSafeState() (ecu/safety/safeSupervisor/src/safeSupervisor/SafeSupervisor.cpp) therefore enters limp home and S1 and then stays: it runs with all interrupts suspended (the lock taken in handle()), so no task, no interrupt and in particular no application runs any more; it walks the safe outputs table again in every round, so S1 stays asserted even if a register is changed in between; and it services the MCU watchdog in every round, because otherwise the watchdog would end the hold after at most 250 ms with a reset into the same loop. An external watchdog (SBC) is deliberately not serviced — with one, its reset and reset counter decide (SM-52). Once, after the first table walk and before the loop, it writes one synchronous console line (SAFE STATE HELD (<event>): outputs off, no reset - power cycle required) so that a board in the hold is not silent. It ends only with a power cycle or a reset from outside. Used at first by exactly two events: WATCHDOG_STARTUP_CHECK_FAILURE (SM-07) and ROM_CHECK_FAILURE (SM-14). State issue 3: also the reset-loop limit (SM-50, SYS-43), a DEVICE_FAULT of the class IDENTITY (SM-34) and a finding of a hardware shut-off path (HW_SHUTOFF with findings, SYS-130; safety manual section 4).

    Decision sys23-halt (user, 2026-09-21): (a) - S1 held without reset stays as implemented. Rejected were S1 + S2 for the ROM check (the bootloader CRC at start covers only corruption after flashing and only projects with the bootloader) and a "blocked start" in no-init RAM (needs NVM-03; may come later as an improvement). The hold services the MCU watchdog. Accepted consequence: a debugger that halts the core during the 6 ms start-up self-test makes the self-test fail and leads into the hold; a power cycle ends it.

    S3 — report and continue. The event is logged. The error memory exists since NVM-03; an S3 event is stored there only where a DTC is defined for it (today the take-back of a foreign safe_off level, SYS-196), while RTE_TIMING_VIOLATION stays a log entry (SYS-113 left it out of the DTC table on purpose). This is the reaction for events where a reset would do more harm than good — today a single RTE timing violation. In issue 1 it was also, unintentionally, the reaction for two events that should have one (finding F1); since SYS-23 those two go to S1 held.

    When S3 is admissible (proposal C18, the answer of iso26262-gap.md (not published, available on request) section 4 point 6): the standard prescribes no reaction; a fault counts as handled only if the safe state is reached within the fault tolerant time interval (FTTI) of the item. "Report and continue" is therefore admissible only where the analysis shows that the detected condition cannot violate a safety requirement within the FTTI, or where the reaction is allocated to another element with its own fault handling time. For the RTE timing violation (SM-05) the reaction is allocated to the application: AoU-18 (extended in issue 3). A latent fault of a safety mechanism is never only reported - that is why F1 ended in S1 held.

    SD — degraded (SYS-117, decisions B1 … B6 of 2026-09-29). For an event on the supervisor's closed list of degradable events (NX_SAFE_DEGRADABLE_EVENTS, safeSupervisor/Degraded.h; today DEVICE_FAULT of a well-formed report whose class is not IDENTITY; the trip of a hardware shut-off path is the second case, below) that the project maps to safe-output groups (project.yaml safety.output_groups, safety.degraded), handle() drives only the entries of the safe outputs table that belong to those groups to their safe state (the same table and the same walk as S1, restricted by an entry mask), then latches the groups, records them in no-init RAM and reports them (log; RTE signal safety.degraded to the application, which a DTC samples for the error memory). No reset, no limp home, every other group, task and mechanism keeps running. Groups only accumulate and are walked again in every safety cycle; leaving S1 releases none; only a power cycle ends SD — a safety reset re-applies the record before the BSP starts, and an implausible record or latch counts as every group degraded. When the last group that still works is degraded, SD becomes S1 without a reset. Faults of the MCU itself (program flow, time base, watchdog, memory, image, kernel, clock, supply, MPU) are never on the list: they put the supervision of the remaining function in doubt; nxconfig refuses such a mapping with its YAML path, and the supervisor refuses it once more at compile time. A device fault on a bus the supervisor regards as lost (F5) is degraded only if every device on that bus is mapped. A project without a mapping gets not one line of SD compiled in. Limits and residuals: fmea.md SM-23; the interplay with the partner supervision (SYS-115) and the active short (SYS-116): partner-and-degraded.md section 4.5.

    The second degradable case: the trip of a hardware shut-off path (SYS-135). Decided by the user as safety responsible in the safety review of SYS-128/SYS-130 (point C1): the latched trip of a hardware shut-off path (SM-58, report HW_SHUTOFF of SYS-130) need not hold S1 for the whole ECU. A project may map it with safety.degraded: [{event: HW_SHUTOFF, groups: [<group>]}] to exactly the safe-output group of the PWM group the path switches off; a trip then degrades only that group, without a reset (the path stays latched until reset, SYS-86 H1), the rest of the ECU keeps running, and the last group escalates to S1 without a reset. The report is on a second closed list of the supervisor (NX_SAFE_DEGRADABLE_REPORTS in safeSupervisor/Degraded.h), because it is no event of SafeSupervisor::Event. Only "tripped and nothing else" is degraded: a finding of the path (switched off, reconfigured, stuck, not latched; SYS-128), a report without context, an unmapped timer and an implausible record keep S1 held - they may stem from a fault of the MCU, which is never degraded. Without the mapping nothing changes. SM-58 itself stays not credited (section 6.5), and its start test needs AoU-37. Issue 3 (CM-07 decision ZA, F-01): the texts released in review SYS-117 (R8) - this paragraph on SD, the last paragraph of section 2.2, AoU-04 and AoU-32 - now name this case; issue 2 described it only in the update block of SYS-135.

    Which event leads to which reaction is decided in exactly one place: ecu/safety/safeSupervisor/src/safeSupervisor/SafeSupervisor.cpp, function handle(). Every mechanism below only raises an event; it never decides the reaction itself. That separation is the load-bearing design decision of the whole concept, and SYS-05 and SYS-06 both re-confirmed it.

    5. How the mechanisms are organised¶

    Three levels, and it matters which one a reader is looking at:

    1. Detection. A piece of code notices something. It raises an event on a monitor of the safe supervisor (trigger(), check(), hit()), optionally with a context word.
    2. Reaction. SafeSupervisor::handle() decides: log and return (S3), only the mapped safe-output groups to their safe state without reset (SD, SYS-117, for the degradable events a project maps), safe state without reset (S1), limp home + safe state held without reset (S1 held, holdSafeState(), SYS-23), or limp home + safe state + reset (S1 + S2).
    3. Recovery. Whatever happens after the reset — start reason, ECU state, the reset counter of the SBC, the configured after_watchdog_reset behaviour.

    Two mechanisms bypass level 1 and 2 on purpose, because the code that would run them cannot be trusted at that moment: the assembler fault handlers and the halt deadman interrupt walk the safe outputs table directly, before anything else, and only afterwards report. That is the reason the table exists as a plain array of address / value / clock-gate words rather than as driver calls.

    6. Catalogue of safety mechanisms¶

    6.1 Program flow and time¶

    SM-01, SM-02, SM-03, SM-04, SM-05, SM-06 and SM-07 - section 6.1 of mechanisms.sdoc.

    Notes.

    • SM-01 and SM-06 overlap on purpose and cover different windows. The watchdog catches a stall of more than 250 ms and answers with a reset; the deadman catches a stall of more than 10 ms and answers by de-energising without a reset. A halt longer than 250 ms hits the watchdog warning interrupt first (IRQ 22, WDOG_EWM), whose handler runs the same safe outputs walk as its first action, before the dump and before any output — measured on the board on 2026-09-18 (EXC_RETURN 0xffffffed, DRV_EN off afterwards).
    • SM-06's residual window. A halt shorter than 10 ms is seen only through the DFSR, and only if the debug probe leaves it standing — the J-Link Commander clears it when it resumes the core (reproduced twice on the board on 2026-09-18). Halt below 10 ms and a probe that clears the DFSR is a gap the user accepted knowingly (decision io32-halt-erkennung, question 2). Software can never react during a halt; only the hardware change documented in gdTmc6200.md (DRV_EN behind an alive latch) closes that.
    • SM-06 on ARMv6-M. Cortex-M0+ has no priority level above the RTOS syscall boundary; the handler takes the highest level there but can be delayed by a critical section for the length of that section. That is a property of the core, not of the mechanism.

    6.2 Memory¶

    SM-10, SM-11, SM-12, SM-13, SM-14, SM-15, SM-16, SM-55, SM-17 and SM-60 (not credited, proposed by NVM-25) - section 6.2 of mechanisms.sdoc.

    Notes.

    • The ECC detection is a status poll, not an exception route. A double-bit error is found at the next safety cycle, not at the access that caused it. The ERM double-fault interrupt vector exists and is wrapped (ERM_double_fault_IRQHandler in IsrWrapper.h), but the reaction path we rely on is the poll. Whether that is sufficient for the fault tolerant time interval of the integrator's goal is an AoU-05 question.
    • SM-14 was not active until SYS-38 (found by SYS-23; confirmed on the board in session d3, 2026-09-21: the list address read 0xFF, a changed byte stopped nothing). Three things were missing against OpenBSW: the reference application calls RomCheck.idle() from its idle handler (app.cpp), neXtBSW's did not; OpenBSW runs tools/safety/crcCheck.py after the link (only with PLATFORM_SUPPORT_ROM_CHECK, a script with a fixed build path and pyelftools, which this repository does not install), neXtBSW had no such step; and neXtBSW removed .checksum from the HEX because at the end of the flash it stretched the CAN download. Had the check run, RomCheck would have taken the erased word 0xFFFFFFFF as the number of areas and walked the addresses 0xFFFFFFFF.
    • No false alarm by construction — a false ROM_CHECK_FAILURE holds the ECU until a power cycle (S1 held): the check is only compiled into an image whose reference the build wrote (NX_ROM_CHECK_IMAGE, set by nx_rom_check_image()), the post-link step verifies the finished HEX (list present, result present and equal to the CRC over the areas as the flash will hold them, gaps as 0xFF like canflash downloads them), and every flash byte of the image is inside an area or is the list itself. The CRC of the tool is checked against a register model of the CRC unit, and the real RomCheck.cpp runs against the same model in the host test rom-check.
    • Interplay with the bootloader (SM-51): the post-link step runs before canflash record, so the validity record covers the image including list and reference (demo: 0x8000…0x320FC, the record's length 0x2A0FC). canflash flash computes its CRC over whatever file it is given, so an image changed after the build passes the bootloader and is caught by SM-14 at run time — that is the board test of SYS-38. The bootloader protects the list and the reference themselves at every start; SM-14 does not cover them (they are not in any area).
    • SM-14's run time is not bounded per call: the CRC runs over all areas in one go (demo: 172 196 bytes at SYS-38, 197 044 bytes at SYS-44) in the idle context. That was finding F3; SYS-20 decided that no slicing is needed at idle priority without a lock (fmea.md 5.1). Since SYS-44 the pass is repeated with the period of the project.
    • No false alarm from flash writes (SYS-44): a write that begins during a pass discards the pass instead of reporting it, and no pass starts while one is open. The write indicator lies in ordinary RAM (library romCheckFlashWrites), so every writer can update it without access to the protected RAM; a wild write into it can only postpone the check, and postponement beyond one period is itself reported.

    6.3 Freedom from interference — what exists and what does not¶

    Today: one address space for all tasks; the MPU separates the safety manager's data and stack from everything else (FUSA_REGION, SAFETY_STACK_REGION), and the fusa gate plus the ISR hooks make sure that window is as narrow as possible. That is protection of the supervisor from the rest — it is not partitioning of the application.

    Missing for ASIL B, and therefore the content of SYS-21:

    • partitions (safety-related / QM) with memory regions and tasks derived from the configuration;
    • an access violation from a QM partition as an event to the safe supervisor rather than a plain fault;
    • temporal freedom from interference: an execution budget per partition, building on SM-05;
    • the communication between partitions: E2E or protected RTE buffers rather than shared variables.

    Until then AoU-17 applies: the integrator treats all application code at the target ASIL, or partitions it himself.

    SYS-61: the coexistence argument per module — which modules are ASIL B (mechanisms, the separating element FreeRTOS with task layout and interrupt lock, and the drivers the mechanisms call), which QM modules are argued free from interference and which rows are still open — is in coexistence.md; its section 7 is the list of evidence SYS-21 has to produce, and its findings CX-1 … CX-3 are interference paths into the reaction itself (logger before the safe state, lifecycle shutdown disabling the watchdog, ETL assert reset without the safe outputs table).

    7.3 What this means for the integrator¶

    The element does not define a fault tolerant time interval (FTTI) — it cannot, because the FTTI belongs to the item it does not know (AoU-01). What it offers instead is the maximum fault handling time interval (FHTI) per class in the table above (proposal C7). The integrator's check is:

    For every safety goal, and for every mechanism this concept lists as contributing to it: is the FTTI of that goal longer than the FHTI in section 7.2 (AoU-05)? The rows of the time bases count as well — in particular the residual risk of a lost SIRC (DF-05).

    Two answers are easy to get wrong and are called out:

    • A reset is not instantaneous. The de-energising happens in microseconds, but the ECU is then out of service for the restart time — bootloader check, clock set-up, start-up of the lifecycle levels. That time is measured on the eval board (section 7.2, F6; safety-manual.md section 4 states the values to use). If the item's safe state requires the ECU to be back, this time counts (finding F6, AoU-05).
    • 10 ms is the floor for anything in the safety task, not a guarantee. Preemption by higher-priority tasks and interrupts adds to it, and the schedule analysis (tools/schedana) is where that must be shown. Whether the safety task is actually the right priority in a given project is an integrator decision (AoU-08).

    • The path split by kind of part (proposal C12, issue 3).     8.2 FreeRTOS¶

    • What it is here. FreeRTOS V10.6.2 from external/openbsw/libs/3rdparty/freeRtos, through OpenBSW's asyncFreeRtos layer, with the port selected per architecture (platforms/common/3rdparty/freertosPort, ports per family).
    • No qualification claim. The kernel in this form carries none. The safety-relevant properties we rely on — that a task runs at its period, that priorities are honoured, that a critical section really masks — are assumed, tested by our own tests, and observed on the board. They are not proven.
    • Qualification path.
    • SAFERTOS (WITTENSTEIN high integrity systems) is the obvious candidate: the same programming model, and it is offered with a certification package for automotive and industrial use. The exact scope of that package and which of our ports and derivatives it covers must be checked with the supplier — it is not verified here. The migration cost is not the API (it is close) but OpenBSW's asyncFreeRtos layer and the two RTOS ports we maintain. It is a commercial licence, which conflicts with the open-source branch (PLAT-46) — a product using it would be a licensed branch.
    • Qualifying FreeRTOS ourselves is possible in principle for ASIL B: a validation argument backed by the kernel's field use plus our own tests against the properties we depend on. It is a large piece of work and it has to be redone at each kernel update.
    • ThreadX is already a second RTOS in the build matrix (target-buildtest-s32k148-threadx) and, as Eclipse ThreadX, has a certification history of its own. Same caveat: the scope must be checked, not assumed.
    • No RTOS at all. A single-loop variant for small safety functions is a real option that the architecture does not forbid, and it would remove the largest single qualification item. It is not on the roadmap today.
    • Route decided (proposal C13, issue 3). The user decided on 2026-09-22 (rtos-u1-anfrage) to stay on FreeRTOS and qualify it as a pre-existing software component (8-12: requirements-based verification including the failure behaviour; structural coverage of the component is expected only at ASIL D); "proven in use" (8-14) is not a realistic route, because it needs field data we do not have. The kernel is also the element that implements the partitioning of SYS-21 and is then ASIL B itself (6-7.4.9).     • What it is here. arm-none-eabi-gcc 11.3.1, the xPack build pinned in tools/ci/versions.toml and set up by python -m tools.ci setup (or taken from NX_ARM_GCC_BIN), C++17, -Os/-Werror, with newlib (stubs in platforms/common/newlib), ETL, and the CMSIS core headers. Issue 3 (F-07): issues 1 and 2 named GCC 11.4 from the NXP S32DS installation, which is no longer installed (dev-environment.md DE1).
    • Components and tools (proposal C14, issue 3). The libraries linked into the image (newlib, ETL, the CMSIS headers) are pre-existing software components (8-12), routed in component-qualification.md section 2; the compiler and the generators are tools (8-11), classified in tool-classification.md.
    • No qualification claim. GCC is not a qualified compiler, the C library is not a qualified library, and the standard C++ library features we use are whatever GCC 11 ships.
    • Qualification path. Two directions.
    • A qualified toolchain. Commercial compilers are offered with functional-safety packages (for example IAR's functional-safety edition, Green Hills, TASKING). Whether any of them covers our core profiles and what the package contains has not been checked; naming them is a starting point, not a recommendation. Cost is a licence plus a port of the build (ours is CMake with GCC-specific flags; the linker scripts and the startup assembler are the awkward part).
    • Qualifying the toolchain we have. The usual shape is a validation suite that exercises the language constructs we actually use, plus the argument that our own tests would catch a mis-compilation. Commercial test suites for this exist (Solid Sands' SuperTest is the one usually named). Not evaluated here. The cheaper partial measure that costs us nothing and is already half in place: the back-to-back checks against the target code that this project already does in places — the disassembly test safe-outputs-order proves that the compiled watchdog vector really walks the table before it calls anything; the ELF checks of SYS-18 prove the static constructor is in .init_array. Extending that pattern to the other mechanisms is a mitigation the integrator can see and check, and it belongs in PLAT-75.
    • Host test toolchain. The host tests are built with zig cc (clang 21.1.0) and the coverage is measured through gcov with a small runtime of our own (TOOL-40). The test toolchain is a different compiler from the target toolchain. That is a strength (two front-ends see the same code) and a weakness (a host test proves the logic, not the emitted target code). The disassembly tests above are the bridge.

    13.1 "Encapsulated" — the interface to the base software¶

    Encapsulated means: a device package decides nothing about the ECU's reaction, exactly like every mechanism of stage 1 (section 3: the reaction is decided in one place). A package detects, and it reports. What it may rely on from the base software, and what the base software expects from it:

    Direction Content Status today
    package → base software a fault or qualifier report to the safe supervisor: an event per device class with a context word (device instance, fault class), raised through the same monitor pattern as every stage-1 mechanism; the reaction (S3, S1, S1 + S2) is chosen in SafeSupervisor::handle(), not in the driver base-software side exists since SYS-29 (SM-34): events DEVICE_REPORT (S3, from any context through the package's DeviceReportBox or from the safety task) and DEVICE_FAULT (switching off, safety task only), context word safeSupervisor/DeviceReport.h with the bus id (F5). No device driver uses it yet (SYS-28); faults still end in the driver's own latch, the log, and — for the gate driver only — the RTE port gd.<device>.faults (IO-25), whose qualifier now carries the supervisor's bus verdict
    package → application the device's measured values with a qualifier (the RTE signal qualifier, SM-30), so that a value from a faulty part is marked, not silently used partly: gd.<device>.faults exists; bcc.<device>.faults and qualifiers for cell values do not
    base software → package the safe state reaches the part: an output the part needs to be de-energised is declared safe_off (stage 1, SM-23); an actuator that is switched by a bus command rather than a pin (balancing) needs a base-software permission that S1 revokes and that the package must hold before commanding safe_off exists; the permission for bus-commanded actuators does not (gap G4)
    base software → package a time budget: the package's supervision cycle runs as a runnable with an RTE deadline (SM-05) exists through the RTE

    The base-software side of this interface — the event, the context format, the permission and its revocation by S1 — is a stage-1 mechanism and is proposed as a task of its own (gap G5, section 10.9). The device-side use of it is SYS-28.

    Update after issue 2: SYS-143 (start test of the active short: power-on only, bounded, enable read back, 2026-09-30)¶

    Decisions E2-E4 of the user as safety responsible in the safety review of SYS-136/SYS-141 (2026-09-30). SYS-141 calls the register-level start test of the short (SM-27, partner-and-degraded.md 5.10) in RteSystem::init(), before the init runnables; SYS-143 changes that call (ecu/application/src/systems/safeState/ SafeStateRte.h, startTests):

    • E4 - power-on only. The shorts are built only when the MCU reports a power-on reset (ecustate:: mcuStartReasons(), wake-up bit POWER). After any other reset (watchdog, pin, software, debugger) the machine may still turn, so nothing is built; the outcome of the power-on test is kept over resets in a record in no-init RAM (.noinit.safeStateTest, passed groups plus a check word, written before it is read after power-on, like the records of SYS-43 and SYS-117). A group keeps its shorts after such a reset only if the record is intact and says it passed; otherwise its shorts are removed until the next power-on (log line of the SAFETY component, qualifier safety.qlf.safe_state.<GROUP> ERROR). A low-voltage reset (RAM content lost) and a start whose reason the MCU cannot tell count as "no record": freewheel only until power-on. Before SYS-143 the removal of a failed test lived in .bss of the service and a warm reset gave the short back without a test.
    • E3 - enable read back. The test stays register level (5.10 step 3 b) and never reaches the bridge: the gate-driver enable bound to the group (board pwm[].gate_driver, a module with RTE access gate_driver) must read off before the short is built and after it is taken back, read through the driver (readGateDriver_<device> of the family integration; the TMC6200 driver verifies DRV_EN against its IOIN register, ENABLE_MISMATCH). An enable that reads on is no passed test (ENABLE_ON), the shorts are removed. The phase voltages stay with IO-64; standstill and the enable of a group without a bound gate driver are AoU-38.
    • E2 - budget. The hold is a busy wait in the first application task before any application task runs. The task that serves the MCU watchdog is above every application task (tasks.yaml above_application, RTE-02) and is not delayed; tasks below it (on the S32K1 port TASK_BSP with the SBC watchdog trigger, the device drivers' supervision and the halt guard's poll, and TASK_BACKGROUND) wait for the sum of all holds. tools/rtegen derives rte::SAFE_STATE_TEST_BUDGET_US = a third of modules.safeLifecycle.watchdog_timeout_ms (the three service opportunities of section 7.1; demo value 250 ms gives 83 333 us) and refuses a larger sum at pwm.<group>.safe_state_test_us; RteSystem.cpp asserts it again at compile time.     Update after issue 2: SYS-125 (low-voltage detect reset in the family port, 2026-09-30)¶

    Decision of the user as safety responsible on 2026-09-30 (anomaly AN-066, finding H9 of hsi-s32k1xx.md): the family port s32k1xx switches the MCU's low-voltage detect reset on instead of leaving the gap to the integrator (AoU-09). Before, a VDD falling below the detect threshold VLVD (2.80 … 3.00 V) was only flagged; the MCU kept running down to the low-voltage reset VLVR (2.50 … 2.70 V), below the specified supply range, and only SM-20 could see the drift of the internal supplies at safety-task speed. Now boardInit() writes PMC_LVDSC1[LVDRE] = 1 in every start, and the hardware resets and holds the MCU until VDD is back above VLVD plus its hysteresis. Added: the catalogue row SM-59 in section 6.4 (a hardware mechanism configured by the base software; not credited until the safety responsible decides, like SM-58), its analysis in fmea.md, the proposed AoU-39 in section 4.3 (supply margin of a 3.3 V board), and the note under SM-20. Effect per board: a 5 V board (the eval board) runs 2 V above the threshold and sees no difference in normal operation; a 3.3 V board keeps a margin of at least 0.3 V only (AoU-39); in the low-power modes VLPR/VLPS the LVD is off and only the LVR acts. Not changed: the reset-loop limit still clears on POR/LVD (a brown-out loop is not counted, SM-59 in fmea.md), the bootloader does not set the bit (after power-on it runs with the reset default until the application starts), and the bit is not re-checked cyclically (rest of H9). Host verified with a register model and the ELF of the demo; board proof open.
